## Question 1: q1_diamond_vs_nitrogen

**Question:** Compare the difference between diamond and nitrogen gas.

### UnderStudy answer

If you see “compare”, talk about both substances. The easiest physical property to compare here is their melting and boiling points.

First, state the structures. Diamond has a giant covalent structure, whereas nitrogen gas has a simple molecular structure.

Diamond has a high melting and boiling point because a lot of energy is required to overcome the strong covalent bonds. Nitrogen gas has a low melting and boiling point because very little energy is required to overcome the weak intermolecular forces of attraction between nitrogen molecules.

Answer to write: Diamond has a giant covalent structure, so it has a high melting and boiling point because a lot of energy is required to overcome the strong covalent bonds. Nitrogen gas has a simple molecular structure, so it has a low melting and boiling point because very little energy is required to overcome the weak intermolecular forces of attraction between molecules.

### Baseline answer

Diamond and nitrogen gas are both made from non-metal atoms, but their structures and properties are very different.

| Feature | Diamond | Nitrogen gas |
|---|---|---|
| Formula | C | N₂ |
| Structure | Giant covalent lattice | Simple molecular substance |
| Bonding | Each carbon atom is strongly covalently bonded to 4 other carbon atoms | Two nitrogen atoms are joined by a strong triple covalent bond |
| Forces between particles | Strong covalent bonds extend throughout the whole structure | Weak intermolecular forces between N₂ molecules |
| State at room temperature | Solid | Gas |
| Melting/boiling point | Very high, because many strong bonds must be broken | Very low, because only weak forces between molecules need to be overcome |
| Hardness | Extremely hard | Not hard; it is a gas |
| Electrical conductivity | Does not conduct electricity because no electrons are free to move | Does not conduct electricity |

In short: **diamond is a giant network of carbon atoms**, making it hard and high-melting, whereas **nitrogen gas consists of small N₂ molecules**, making it a gas with low melting and boiling points.

## Question 2: q2_metal_conductivity_malleability

**Question:** Explain why metals conduct electricity and why they are malleable.

### UnderStudy answer

Metals have a giant metallic lattice structure with an orderly arrangement.

Metals conduct electricity because the sea of delocalised electrons acts as charge carriers. These delocalised electrons are mobile, so they can move through the metal and carry electrical charge, even in the solid state.

Metals are malleable because layers of metal cations can easily slide past each other in the orderly giant metallic lattice structure.

### Baseline answer

Metals have a structure called a **metallic lattice**. Their atoms are arranged in regular layers, and they share a “sea” of **delocalised electrons**.

### Why metals conduct electricity
In a metal, the outer electrons are not attached to one particular atom. They can move freely through the lattice.

When a voltage is applied, these mobile electrons flow through the metal, carrying electric charge. That is why metals such as copper and aluminium are good electrical conductors.

### Why metals are malleable
Malleable means a material can be hammered or pressed into shape without breaking.

Metal atoms are arranged in layers. When a force is applied, the layers can **slide over one another**. The delocalised electrons still attract the positive metal ions and hold the structure together, even after the layers move.

This means metals bend or change shape instead of shattering.

## Question 3: q3_group1_vs_transition_metals

**Question:** What is the difference between Group 1 metals and transition metals?

### UnderStudy answer

This comparison belongs to the topic Patterns in the Periodic Table.

For bonding and structure, the key difference to remember is:

Group 1 metals are generally soft and have relatively low melting and boiling points, whereas transition metals are generally harder and have high melting and boiling points.

Both are metals, so they have a giant metallic lattice structure and conduct electricity because the sea of delocalised electrons acts as charge carriers.

### Baseline answer

**Group 1 metals** and **transition metals** are both metals, but they have different properties because of their electron arrangements.

| Feature | Group 1 metals (alkali metals) | Transition metals |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Lithium, sodium, potassium | Iron, copper, nickel, titanium |
| Position on periodic table | Far-left column | Middle block of the table |
| Outer electrons | 1 outer-shell electron | Have electrons in a partly filled inner *d* shell (in their atoms or ions) |
| Ions formed | Usually form **+1 ions** only, e.g. Na⁺ | Can form ions with different charges, e.g. Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ |
| Reactivity | Very reactive, especially with water and oxygen | Usually less reactive; some, such as gold, are very unreactive |
| Physical properties | Soft, low density, relatively low melting points | Usually hard, dense, strong, and have high melting points |
| Compounds | Usually white or colourless | Often make coloured compounds |
| Catalysts | Less commonly used as catalysts | Many act as catalysts, such as iron in the Haber process |

For example, **sodium** (Group 1) is soft enough to cut with a knife and reacts vigorously with water. **Iron** (a transition metal) is strong and useful for building, and can form different ions and coloured compounds.

In short: **Group 1 metals are very reactive metals that usually form +1 ions, while transition metals are usually stronger, less reactive metals that can form different ions and often make coloured compounds.**

## Question 4: q4_nacl_structure_bonding

**Question:** Describe the structure and bonding in sodium chloride.

### UnderStudy answer

Sodium chloride has a giant ionic lattice structure with an orderly arrangement of sodium ions and chloride ions.

There are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the sodium cations, Na⁺, and chloride anions, Cl⁻, throughout the lattice.

### Baseline answer

Sodium chloride has a **giant ionic lattice structure**.

- Sodium atoms each lose one electron to form positive **sodium ions, Na⁺**.
- Chlorine atoms each gain one electron to form negative **chloride ions, Cl⁻**.
- The oppositely charged ions are held together by strong **electrostatic attractions**. This is called **ionic bonding**.

In the solid, the ions are arranged in a regular three-dimensional repeating pattern. Each sodium ion is surrounded by chloride ions, and each chloride ion is surrounded by sodium ions.

Because the ionic attractions are strong, sodium chloride has a high melting and boiling point. It does not conduct electricity as a solid because the ions cannot move, but it does conduct when molten or dissolved in water because the ions are free to move.

## Question 5: q5_nacl_conductivity

**Question:** Why does sodium chloride conduct electricity when molten but not when solid?

### UnderStudy answer

Sodium chloride has a giant ionic lattice structure, with strong electrostatic forces of attraction between sodium ions and chloride ions.

When solid, the ions are fixed in the lattice, so there are no mobile ions to act as charge carriers. Therefore, solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity.

When molten, the lattice breaks down and the sodium ions and chloride ions are free to move. These mobile ions act as charge carriers, so molten sodium chloride conducts electricity.

### Baseline answer

In solid sodium chloride, the sodium ions (\(\text{Na}^+\)) and chloride ions (\(\text{Cl}^-\)) are locked in a rigid crystal lattice. Although they are charged, they cannot move, so they cannot carry an electric current.

When sodium chloride is melted, the lattice breaks apart. The \(\text{Na}^+\) and \(\text{Cl}^-\) ions are then free to move through the liquid. These moving charged particles carry electricity, so molten sodium chloride conducts.

## Question 6: q6_diamond_vs_graphite_conductivity

**Question:** Compare the electrical conductivity of diamond and graphite.

### UnderStudy answer

Diamond and graphite are both giant covalent structures made of carbon, but they have different electrical conductivity.

Diamond does not conduct electricity in any state because there are no mobile ions or electrons to act as charge carriers.

Graphite conducts electricity because each carbon atom is bonded to three other carbon atoms, leaving delocalised electrons that can act as charge carriers.

### Baseline answer

Diamond does not conduct electricity because each carbon atom uses all four of its outer electrons to form strong covalent bonds. There are no free electrons available to carry an electric current.

Graphite does conduct electricity, especially along its layers. Each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds, leaving one electron free to move through the layers. These mobile electrons carry electrical charge.

So:

- **Diamond:** electrical insulator  
- **Graphite:** electrical conductor due to delocalised electrons

## Question 7: q7_co2_low_melting_point

**Question:** Explain why simple molecular substances like carbon dioxide have low melting points.

### UnderStudy answer

Carbon dioxide has a simple molecular structure. There are weak intermolecular forces of attraction between its molecules, so very little energy is required to overcome these forces. Therefore, carbon dioxide has a low melting point.

### Baseline answer

Simple molecular substances such as carbon dioxide are made of small, separate molecules.

The atoms within each molecule are held together by strong covalent bonds. However, the forces *between* the molecules are much weaker. These are called intermolecular forces.

When carbon dioxide melts, the covalent bonds inside each CO₂ molecule do not break. Only the weak forces between neighbouring molecules need to be overcome. This requires relatively little energy, so carbon dioxide has a low melting point.

## Question 9: q9_metal_structure_conductivity

**Question:** Describe the structure of a typical metal and explain why metals are good conductors of electricity.

### UnderStudy answer

A typical metal has a giant metallic lattice structure with an orderly arrangement of metal cations surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons.

Metals are good conductors of electricity because the sea of delocalised electrons acts as charge carriers. These electrons are mobile and can move through the structure, carrying electrical charge.

### Baseline answer

A typical metal has a **giant lattice structure**. Its atoms are arranged in closely packed layers.

In a metal:

- The atoms lose some of their outer electrons.
- This leaves behind **positive metal ions** arranged in a regular lattice.
- The lost outer electrons become **delocalised**—they are free to move throughout the structure.

The positive ions are held together by strong electrostatic attraction to the “sea” of negative delocalised electrons. This is called **metallic bonding**.

Metals conduct electricity well because their delocalised electrons can move easily through the lattice. When a voltage is applied, these electrons flow in a particular direction, carrying electrical charge through the metal.

## Question 10: q10_metals_malleable_ionic_brittle

**Question:** Explain why metals are malleable but ionic compounds are brittle.

### UnderStudy answer

Okay, compare the structures first. Metals have a giant metallic lattice structure, whereas ionic compounds have a giant ionic lattice structure.

In metals, layers of metal cations can easily slide past each other in the orderly giant metallic lattice structure. The sea of delocalized electrons continues to hold the metal cations together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction. Therefore, metals are malleable: they can be hammered into shape without breaking.

In an ionic compound, when a force causes layers of ions to shift, ions with the same charge may become next to each other. There is strong repulsion between these like charges, so the giant ionic lattice structure breaks apart. Therefore, ionic compounds are brittle.

Answer to write: Metals are malleable because layers of metal cations can easily slide past each other in the orderly giant metallic lattice structure, while the sea of delocalized electrons continues to hold the structure together. Ionic compounds are brittle because when layers shift, ions with the same charge are brought next to each other and repel strongly, causing the giant ionic lattice structure to break.

### Baseline answer

Metals are **malleable** because their atoms are arranged in layers held together by metallic bonding. In a metal, positive metal ions are surrounded by a “sea” of mobile electrons. When a force is applied, the layers of ions can slide past one another, but the mobile electrons still hold the ions together. This lets metals be hammered or bent into new shapes without breaking.

Ionic compounds are **brittle** because they contain a rigid lattice of alternating positive and negative ions. When force makes the layers shift, ions with the same charge can end up next to each other. Since like charges repel strongly, the lattice splits apart instead of bending. That is why crystals such as sodium chloride tend to crack or shatter when hit.

## Question 11: q11_is_graphite_a_molecule

**Question:** Is graphite a molecule? Explain your answer.

### UnderStudy answer

No. Graphite is not a molecule; it is a giant covalent structure with layers.

A molecule is a discrete, fixed-size group of atoms, such as one carbon dioxide molecule, CO₂. Graphite does not have separate, countable units like this. It is one continuous structure in which carbon atoms are covalently bonded to three other carbon atoms in layers.

So graphite is a form of elemental carbon with a giant covalent structure, not a molecule.

### Baseline answer

No. Graphite is not made of separate, individual molecules.

Graphite is a **giant covalent structure** (also called a network solid). It consists of huge layers of carbon atoms joined together in hexagonal patterns. Each carbon atom is strongly bonded to three others within a layer.

The layers are held together only by weak forces, so they can slide over one another. This is why graphite is soft and can be used in pencils.

Although graphite is made only of carbon and can be written as **C**, that does not mean it is a molecule like CO₂ or H₂O. A molecule has a fixed, small number of atoms; graphite is one extended network containing an enormous number of carbon atoms.

## Question 12: q12_mgo_vs_so2_properties

**Question:** Compare the properties of magnesium oxide and sulfur dioxide.

### UnderStudy answer

Okay, for a comparison question, talk about both substances. The easiest physical properties to compare are their melting and boiling points, and electrical conductivity.

Magnesium oxide is an ionic compound with a giant ionic lattice structure. Sulfur dioxide has a simple molecular structure. Their different structures and bonding give them different properties.

Answer to write:

Magnesium oxide has a giant ionic lattice structure and has high melting and boiling points because a lot of energy is required to overcome the strong electrostatic forces of attraction between magnesium cations and oxide anions. Sulfur dioxide has a simple molecular structure and has low melting and boiling points because very little energy is required to overcome the weak intermolecular forces of attraction between molecules.

Molten or aqueous magnesium oxide conducts electricity because there are mobile ions to act as charge carriers. Sulfur dioxide does not conduct electricity because there are no mobile ions or electrons to act as charge carriers.

### Baseline answer

| Property | Magnesium oxide (MgO) | Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) |
|---|---|---|
| Type of substance | Ionic compound | Simple molecular (covalent) compound |
| Elements involved | Metal + non-metal | Two non-metals |
| Structure | Giant ionic lattice of Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions | Small molecules with covalent bonds |
| State at room temperature | White solid | Colourless gas with a sharp, choking smell |
| Melting/boiling point | Very high, because strong electrostatic forces hold ions together | Low, because only weak intermolecular forces act between molecules |
| Electrical conductivity | Does not conduct as a solid, but conducts when molten because ions can move | Does not conduct electricity because it has no mobile charged particles |
| Effect on water | Reacts slowly with water to form magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)₂ | Dissolves in water to form sulfurous acid, H₂SO₃ |
| Acid-base behaviour | Basic oxide; neutralises acids | Acidic oxide; neutralises alkalis |
| Example reaction | MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O | SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O |

So, magnesium oxide is a high-melting, ionic, basic solid, while sulfur dioxide is a low-boiling, covalent, acidic gas.

## Question 13: q13_transition_metal_reactivity_trends

**Question:** What are the trends in reactivity across the transition metals?

### UnderStudy answer

Trends in reactivity across transition metals belong to Patterns in the Periodic Table, rather than chemical bonding and structure.

For this topic, remember only that transition metals are generally less reactive than Group 1 metals. The detailed trend across the transition metals should be learnt under Patterns in the Periodic Table.

### Baseline answer

Across a transition-metal series (for example, Sc to Cu in the first row), there is a **general tendency for metallic reactivity to decrease from left to right**, but the trend is **not smooth**.

### Broad pattern
- **Early transition metals** such as scandium, titanium, and vanadium are generally more electropositive. They tend to lose electrons more readily and form compounds with oxygen, halogens, etc.
- **Middle metals** such as chromium, manganese, iron, cobalt, and nickel have intermediate reactivity.
- **Later metals** such as copper, silver, platinum, and gold are much less reactive. Copper does not react with dilute acids to produce hydrogen, while gold and platinum are especially unreactive.

For example, a simplified section of the metal reactivity series is:

\[
\text{Zn} > \text{Fe} > \text{Co} > \text{Ni} > \text{H} > \text{Cu} > \text{Ag} > \text{Au}
\]

(Zinc is often included in this comparison, although it is not strictly a transition metal by the usual definition.)

### Why the pattern is irregular
Transition metals have partially filled \(d\) orbitals, so their electron arrangements and bonding vary in complicated ways. This means there is no perfectly regular left-to-right trend.

Other factors also matter:

- **Oxide coatings (passivation):** Titanium, chromium, and aluminium can seem unreactive because they form a thin, protective oxide layer. For example, titanium is quite reactive chemically but is protected by titanium oxide.
- **Reaction conditions:** A metal may react with oxygen when heated but not with cold water. Some react with acids but not with water.
- **Oxidation state:** Transition metals can form several ions, so their reactivity can change depending on which oxidation state is involved.

### Down a transition-metal group
There is also **no simple universal trend down a group**. Heavier transition metals often form stronger metallic bonds and can be less easily oxidised, but there are many exceptions. For instance, platinum and gold are far less reactive than nickel, even though they are in related transition-metal groups.

So the key idea is:

> **Transition-metal reactivity generally decreases toward the right of a period, but the trend has many exceptions because of \(d\)-electron effects and protective oxide layers.**
