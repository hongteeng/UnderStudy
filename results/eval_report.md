## Question 1: q1_diamond_vs_nitrogen

**Question:** Compare the difference between diamond and nitrogen gas.

### UnderStudy answer

When you see **compare**, you must talk about both substances. The easiest physical property to compare here is their melting and boiling points.

First, identify the structures. Diamond has a **giant covalent structure**, whereas nitrogen gas has a **simple molecular structure**.

Diamond has strong covalent bonds throughout the structure. To melt or boil diamond, you need to overcome these strong covalent bonds, so a lot of energy is required.

Nitrogen gas consists of small, discrete N₂ molecules. Between the molecules, there are only weak intermolecular forces of attraction, so very little energy is required to overcome them.

### Answer to write

Diamond has a giant covalent structure, so it has a high melting and boiling point because a lot of energy is required to overcome the strong covalent bonds. Nitrogen gas has a simple molecular structure, so it has a low melting and boiling point because very little energy is required to overcome the weak intermolecular forces of attraction between molecules.

### Baseline answer

Diamond and nitrogen gas are both made from non-metal atoms and involve covalent bonds, but their structures are very different.

| Feature | Diamond | Nitrogen gas (N₂) |
|---|---|---|
| Elements present | Carbon only | Nitrogen only |
| Structure | Giant covalent lattice (a huge 3D network) | Simple molecular substance, made of separate N₂ molecules |
| Bonds | Each carbon atom forms 4 strong covalent bonds to other carbon atoms | Each N₂ molecule has a strong triple covalent bond between 2 nitrogen atoms |
| Forces between particles | Strong covalent bonds throughout the whole structure | Weak forces between separate N₂ molecules |
| State at room temperature | Solid | Gas |
| Melting/boiling point | Extremely high | Very low |
| Hardness | Extremely hard | Not hard; it is a gas |
| Electrical conductivity | Does not conduct electricity because no free electrons can move | Does not conduct electricity |

The key difference is that diamond has strong covalent bonds extending throughout a giant structure, whereas nitrogen gas consists of small molecules with weak forces between them.

## Question 2: q2_metal_conductivity_malleability

**Question:** Explain why metals conduct electricity and why they are malleable.

### UnderStudy answer

Metals have a **giant metallic lattice structure**: an orderly arrangement of metal cations surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons.

They conduct electricity because the sea of delocalised electrons can move through the structure and act as **charge carriers**, even when the metal is solid.

Metals are **malleable and ductile** because the layers of metal cations in the orderly giant metallic lattice structure can easily slide past each other without breaking the structure.

### Baseline answer

Metals have a structure made of closely packed positive metal ions surrounded by a “sea” of delocalised electrons.

- **Why metals conduct electricity:**  
  The outer electrons are not fixed to one atom. They can move freely through the metal lattice. When a voltage is applied, these mobile electrons flow and carry electrical charge.

- **Why metals are malleable:**  
  Metal ions are arranged in layers. When a force is applied, the layers can slide over each other without the structure breaking apart. The delocalised electrons still attract the positive ions and hold the metal together, even after the layers move.

This is why metals can be bent, hammered, or shaped into sheets instead of shattering.

## Question 3: q3_group1_vs_transition_metals

**Question:** What is the difference between Group 1 metals and transition metals?

### UnderStudy answer

The detailed differences between Group 1 metals and transition metals belong to **Patterns in the Periodic Table**, not bonding and structure.

For bonding and structure, remember that metals generally have a **giant metallic lattice structure** with strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the metal cations and the sea of delocalised electrons. This explains why metals conduct electricity and are malleable and ductile.

The comparison of their melting and boiling points, hardness, and other differences is covered under **Patterns in the Periodic Table**.

### Baseline answer

**Group 1 metals** and **transition metals** are both metals, but they have different properties.

### Group 1 metals (alkali metals)
Examples: **lithium, sodium, potassium**

- Found in the **first column** of the periodic table.
- Have **one electron in their outer shell**.
- Very **reactive**, especially with water and oxygen.
- Usually **soft** and can be cut with a knife.
- Have relatively **low melting points** and densities.
- Form ions with a **+1 charge**.
- Their compounds are usually **white or colourless**.

Example: sodium reacts vigorously with water to make sodium hydroxide and hydrogen gas.

### Transition metals
Examples: **iron, copper, nickel, zinc**

- Found in the **middle block** of the periodic table.
- Usually less reactive than Group 1 metals.
- Typically **hard, strong, and dense**.
- Have **high melting points**.
- Often form **coloured compounds**.
- Can form ions with **different charges**, such as iron forming Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺.
- Many are useful as **catalysts**, meaning they speed up chemical reactions.
- Many can be used to make alloys, such as steel.

### Quick comparison

| Property | Group 1 metals | Transition metals |
|---|---|---|
| Reactivity | Very reactive | Usually less reactive |
| Hardness | Soft | Hard and strong |
| Melting point | Low | High |
| Ions formed | Usually +1 only | Can have different charges |
| Compound colours | Usually colourless/white | Often coloured |
| Catalysts | Rarely | Often |

So, Group 1 metals are soft and extremely reactive, while transition metals are usually stronger, less reactive, and have more varied chemical properties.
