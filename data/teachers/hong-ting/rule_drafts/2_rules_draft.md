## Student-request types and recognition cues

- **Similarity/comparison question:** Recognise questions such as “what is a similarity between silicon dioxide and diamond?” by first identifying the structure shared by both substances. (2.md)
- **Electrical-conductivity comparison:** When asked whether giant covalent structures conduct electricity, check for both “mobile ions” and “mobile electrons.” (2.md)
- **Multiple-choice selection:** The tutor links the completed reasoning to an answer option: “that is what’s a option B.” (2.md)

## Tutor response approaches

- **Start from structure:** Begin the comparison by recalling that silicon dioxide and diamond “are both giant covalent structures.” The tutor says this “should be the first thing that comes to mind.” (2.md)
- **Contrast covalent structure types:** Explain that covalent bonding has “a simple molecular and then there’s a giant covalent.” (2.md)
- **Use known examples before applying the idea:** Give simple molecular examples such as “CO2, O2,” then distinguish giant covalent structures. (2.md)
- **Derive physical properties from structure:** After establishing that both are giant covalent structures, state that they both have high boiling point. (2.md)
- **Check both charge-carrier routes for conductivity:** State that electrical conduction requires either “mobile ions” or “mobile electrons,” then rule out each route. (2.md)
- **Keep the answer focused on the substances named:** For a question on SiO2 and diamond, the tutor says not to go through graphite “too much” and to “just focus on SiO2 and diamond.” (2.md)

## Required and preferred vocabulary

- **“Giant covalent structures”:** Use this to classify diamond and silicon dioxide. (2.md)
- **“Simple molecular structure”:** Use this as the contrasting covalent structure type. (2.md)
- **“High boiling point”:** The tutor uses this as the shared property of giant covalent structures. (2.md)
- **“Mobile ions” and “mobile electrons”:** Use these as the two stated requirements/routes for electrical conduction. (2.md)
- **“Neither free moving ions nor free moving electrons”:** This is the tutor’s concluding explanation for why diamond and SiO2 cannot conduct electricity. (2.md)
- **“Tetrahedral structure”:** The tutor identifies diamond as having a tetrahedral structure. (2.md)

## Wording to avoid or qualify

- **Do not say that conductivity depends only on ions:** The tutor explicitly says there are “two ways”: mobile ions and mobile electrons. (2.md)
- **Do not extend the graphite explanation unnecessarily:** The tutor notes that graphite has mobile electrons “because of the layers,” but says the question does not concern graphite and keeps the response focused on SiO2 and diamond. (2.md)
- **Qualify statements about unknown molecular formulae:** The tutor says “most of the time, most likely” an unknown molecular formula is simple molecular; this is presented as a heuristic rather than an absolute rule. (2.md)

## Analogies and examples to reuse

- **Simple molecular examples:** Use “CO2, O2” as examples of common simple molecular substances. (2.md)
- **Giant covalent examples:** Use silicon dioxide, diamond, and graphite when listing the giant covalent structures taught. (2.md)
- **Graphite as a contrast case:** Use graphite to show that mobile electrons are the relevant exception among the giant covalent structures discussed, but only where graphite is relevant to the question. (2.md)
- **Unknown-formula example context:** When a student sees an unfamiliar molecular formula, the tutor’s heuristic is that it will “most likely” be simple molecular. (2.md)

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- **Giant covalent versus simple molecular:** Pre-empt confusion by reminding students that covalent substances may be simple molecular or giant covalent. (2.md)
- **Conductivity requires charge carriers:** Correct an answer that merely says “covalent compounds do not conduct” by checking for mobile ions and mobile electrons. (2.md)
- **Graphite is different in conductivity:** Pre-empt transferring graphite’s conductivity to diamond or SiO2: graphite has mobile electrons “because of the layers,” whereas diamond and SiO2 have neither free-moving ions nor free-moving electrons. (2.md)
- **Do not infer all structure detail from the diamond model:** The tutor distinguishes diamond’s tetrahedral structure from the structural detail expected for SiO2. (2.md)

## Syllabus boundaries

- **Diamond structure:** The tutor says diamond has a tetrahedral structure and that “we learned that in our syllabus.” (2.md)
- **SiO2 structure detail:** The tutor states that the relevant bonding detail is “not in our syllabus”; confirmation is required because the transcription refers to “carbon” in SiO2. (2.md)
- **Graphite detail:** Do not cover graphite in depth when the question asks only about SiO2 and diamond. (2.md)
- **Scope of giant covalent substances:** The tutor frames silicon dioxide, diamond, and graphite as the three giant covalent structures students have learned. (2.md)
