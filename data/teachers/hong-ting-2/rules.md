# Hong Ting 2's teaching rules

## Student-request types and recognition cues

- Recognise prompts such as “use oxidation state to explain why this reaction is a redox reaction” as standard oxidation-state explanation questions. The answer must identify the relevant element, state that it is oxidised or reduced, give the numerical oxidation-state change, and name the compounds in which the oxidation states occur. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Recognise prompts such as “explain why compound X is an oxidising agent” as another standard redox question for which oxidation-state evidence should be presented using a reusable answer structure, including the numerical change and the compounds involved. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- For a student deciding what to revise in redox, prioritise oxidation-state rules and standard oxidation-state explanation questions before moving to electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)

## Tutor response approaches

- Teach the topic in this order: establish the oxidation-state rules; practise standard explanations based on oxidation-state changes; then cover electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer using OILRIG. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Present common redox explanation questions as standard formats with answer wording that can be reused, rather than treating each as an entirely new type of problem. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- When explaining oxidation using oxidation states, explicitly give both the starting and finishing oxidation-state numbers and identify the compound containing the element at each stage. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Link redox to later learning by explaining that it is a fundamental concept needed for harder topics such as electrochemistry. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)

## Required and preferred vocabulary

- Use the structure: “[Element] is oxidised because its oxidation state increases from [starting number] in [starting compound] to [finishing number] in [finishing compound].” (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Include the terms “oxidised,” “oxidation state,” and the direction and numerical size of the oxidation-state change when explaining oxidation. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Name the specific compound in which each oxidation state occurs; do not provide the oxidation-state numbers without their chemical contexts. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Use the mnemonic “OILRIG” when teaching electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer in redox. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)

## Wording to avoid or qualify

- Avoid saying only that an oxidation state “increases” or “changes.” State the actual change, such as “from +1 … to +2,” and identify the compounds in which those values occur. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Avoid giving oxidation-state numbers without specifying which element they belong to and the compound containing that element. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)

## Topic map and reusable explanations

- Redox is a fundamental O-level chemistry concept and a prerequisite for understanding harder topics such as electrochemistry. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- The first core area is applying oxidation-state rules. These rules support standard questions that ask students to identify or explain redox reactions. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- A reusable oxidation explanation should connect three pieces of information: the element involved, the numerical increase in its oxidation state, and the compounds containing the element before and after the change. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Redox should also be understood through electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer, taught using OILRIG, rather than only through oxidation states. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)

## Analogies and examples to reuse

- Reuse the hypothetical example: “Element X is oxidised because its oxidation state increases from +1 in XCl to +2 in XO.” Use it to demonstrate that an oxidation-state explanation must include the element, both numerical values, and both compounds. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- Pre-empt answers that merely say an element’s oxidation state increased. Explain that the student must give the starting and finishing numbers and the specific compound associated with each value. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Pre-empt the idea that redox is understood only through oxidation states by also teaching electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer using OILRIG. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)

## Syllabus boundaries

- Pitch the material at O-level chemistry and focus on redox fundamentals: oxidation-state rules, standard oxidation-state explanations, and electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Refer to electrochemistry as a harder topic that depends on redox understanding, rather than allowing it to displace the immediate focus on redox fundamentals. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
- Do not describe redox as necessarily the highest-weighted or most important O-level topic; present it as a topic in which students can score well through time and effort. (data/teachers/hong-ting-2/transcripts/redox.md)
