## Question 2: q2_metal_conductivity_malleability

Explain why metals conduct electricity and why they are malleable.

### Answer A

Metals have a giant metallic lattice structure.

For electrical conductivity, there are strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the metal cations and a sea of delocalised electrons. The sea of delocalised electrons can move and conduct electricity.

For malleability, layers of metal ions can slide past one another while metallic bonding is maintained. Therefore, a pure metal can be bent or hammered into shape without breaking.

### Answer B

Metals have a structure called **metallic bonding**.

- Metal atoms form a lattice of positive metal ions.
- Their outer electrons are not held by just one atom. Instead, they are **delocalised**—free to move throughout the metal.

### Why metals conduct electricity
Electric current is the movement of charged particles. In a metal, the delocalised electrons can move through the lattice when a voltage is applied. Because these electrons carry negative charge, they transfer electrical energy easily.

### Why metals are malleable
Malleable means a material can be hammered or pressed into shape without breaking.

In a metal, layers of positive ions can slide past one another. The delocalised electrons still attract and hold the ions together even after the layers move. Therefore, the metal bends or changes shape instead of cracking apart.

## Question 4: q4_nacl_structure_bonding

Describe the structure and bonding in sodium chloride.

### Answer A

Sodium chloride, NaCl, is an **ionic compound** made from sodium and chlorine atoms.

- A sodium atom loses one electron to form a positive ion, **Na⁺**.
- A chlorine atom gains that electron to form a negative ion, **Cl⁻**.
- The oppositely charged ions attract each other strongly. This attraction is called an **ionic bond**.

In solid sodium chloride, the ions are arranged in a regular, repeating **giant ionic lattice** rather than as separate NaCl molecules. Each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ ion is surrounded by six Na⁺ ions.

Because there are strong electrostatic attractions throughout the lattice, sodium chloride has a high melting and boiling point. It conducts electricity when molten or dissolved in water because the ions can then move, but not when solid because the ions are fixed in place.

### Answer B

Sodium chloride has a giant ionic lattice structure.

There is a strong electrostatic force of attraction between the Na⁺ cations and Cl⁻ anions.

## Question 5: q5_nacl_conductivity

Why does sodium chloride conduct electricity when molten but not when solid?

### Answer A

In solid sodium chloride, the sodium ions (\(\text{Na}^+\)) and chloride ions (\(\text{Cl}^-\)) are locked in a rigid crystal lattice. They have electric charge, but they cannot move, so they cannot carry an electric current.

When sodium chloride is molten (melted), the crystal lattice breaks apart. The \(\text{Na}^+\) and \(\text{Cl}^-\) ions are free to move through the liquid. Because moving charged particles carry electricity, molten sodium chloride conducts electricity.

### Answer B

First of all, sodium chloride has a giant ionic lattice structure.

There is a strong electrostatic force of attraction between the Na⁺ cations and the Cl⁻ anions.

When sodium chloride is solid, the ions vibrate about fixed positions and are not free to move, so it does not conduct electricity.

When sodium chloride is molten, the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are free-moving and can act as charge carriers, so it conducts electricity.

### Answer A

So, graphite is a conductor, while diamond is an electrical insulator.

## Question 10: q10_metals_malleable_ionic_brittle

Explain why metals are malleable but ionic compounds are brittle.

### Answer A

Metals are **malleable** because their atoms are held together by **metallic bonding**. In a metal, positive metal ions are arranged in layers and surrounded by a “sea” of delocalized electrons.

When a force is applied, the layers of metal ions can slide past one another. The mobile electrons still attract and hold the ions together, so the metal changes shape instead of breaking.

Ionic compounds are **brittle** because they are made of a rigid lattice of positive and negative ions. When force makes the layers shift, ions with the same charge can end up next to each other. Since like charges repel strongly, the lattice splits apart or cracks.

So:

- **Metals:** layers slide while bonding remains → malleable.  
- **Ionic compounds:** shifting brings like charges together → repulsion causes fracture.

When a force is applied, layers of ions may shift so that ions with the same charge are next to each other. They repel strongly, causing the lattice to break or shatter. Therefore, ionic compounds are brittle.

## Question 13: q13_transition_metal_reactivity_trends

What are the trends in reactivity across the transition metals?

### Answer A

“Reactivity” for transition metals does not follow one perfectly smooth trend, because it depends on what reaction you mean: reacting with water, acids, oxygen, or forming compounds.

### Across a transition series (left to right)
There is a **general decrease in metallic reactivity** from the left side to the right side.

- Metals on the left, such as **scandium, titanium, and vanadium**, are relatively electropositive and tend to lose electrons more easily.
- Metals in the middle, such as **iron, cobalt, and nickel**, are moderately reactive.
- Metals near the right, such as **copper, silver, gold, and platinum**, are much less reactive. They are often called **noble metals** because they resist oxidation and corrosion.

For example, in the first transition series:

\[
\text{Sc} \rightarrow \text{Ti} \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow \text{Fe} \rightarrow \text{Co} \rightarrow \text{Ni} \rightarrow \text{Cu}
\]

the broad trend is toward lower reactivity, although there are exceptions.

### Why is the trend not perfectly regular?
Transition metals have similar outer electron structures, but their \(d\) electrons affect bonding and oxidation states. As you move across the series:

- nuclear charge increases,
- atoms hold electrons more strongly,
- it generally becomes harder for the metal to lose electrons and form positive ions.

However, factors such as the stability of particular oxidation states and surface oxide layers can change the pattern.

### Important exception: protective oxide layers
Some reactive metals appear unreactive because they quickly form a tough oxide coating.

- **Aluminium** (not a transition metal) is a classic example.
- **Titanium** and **chromium** also form protective oxide layers.

For instance, titanium is quite reactive thermodynamically, but its oxide coating prevents it from reacting quickly with air or water.

### Down a transition-metal group
There is no single simple trend down every group, but metals lower in some groups are often **less reactive and more “noble.”**

Examples:

- Group 11: \(\text{Cu} > \text{Ag} > \text{Au}\) in reactivity, so gold is extremely unreactive.
- Group 10: nickel is generally more reactive than palladium and platinum.

### Summary
- **Across a period:** reactivity generally decreases from left to right.
- **Toward the right:** metals become less likely to lose electrons and more resistant to corrosion.
- **The trend has exceptions** because of oxide coatings, \(d\)-electron arrangements, and unusually stable oxidation states.

### Answer B

This goes beyond the material we have reviewed, so do double-check it with your notes.

There is no simple, regular trend in reactivity across the transition metals.

In general:
- Transition metals are less reactive than Group 1 and Group 2 metals.
- Their reactivity varies from metal to metal rather than steadily increasing or decreasing across the period.
- Metals towards the middle of the transition series often show variable oxidation states and can be quite reactive in some reactions.
- Metals near the end, such as copper, silver and gold, are generally less reactive and may occur uncombined in nature.

So, unlike the clear trends in Group 1 or Group 7, transition-metal reactivity is irregular across the series.

## Question 14: q14_redox_mg_cuo_oxidation_states

Use oxidation states to explain why the reaction between magnesium and copper(II) oxide is a redox reaction.

### Answer A

Mg + CuO → MgO + Cu

Magnesium is oxidized because its oxidation state increased from 0 in Mg to plus 2 in MgO.

Copper is reduced because its oxidation state decreased from plus 2 in CuO to 0 in Cu.

Therefore, both oxidation and reduction happen, so this is a redox reaction.

### Answer B

In the reaction:

\[
\text{Mg} + \text{CuO} \rightarrow \text{MgO} + \text{Cu}
\]

the oxidation states change:

- Magnesium starts as Mg, so its oxidation state is **0**.
- In magnesium oxide, MgO, magnesium is **+2**.

Magnesium’s oxidation state increases from 0 to +2, so magnesium is **oxidised** because it loses electrons.

- Copper in copper(II) oxide, CuO, has an oxidation state of **+2**.
- Copper metal, Cu, has an oxidation state of **0**.

Copper’s oxidation state decreases from +2 to 0, so copper is **reduced** because it gains electrons.

Because oxidation and reduction happen at the same time, this reaction is a **redox reaction**.

## Question 17: q17_redox_electron_transfer_oil_rig

Explain oxidation and reduction in terms of electron transfer, using the mnemonic OIL RIG.

### Answer A

**OIL RIG** is a mnemonic to remember electron transfer in redox reactions:

- **OIL** = **Oxidation Is Loss** of electrons  
- **RIG** = **Reduction Is Gain** of electrons  

So:

- When an atom, ion, or molecule **loses electrons**, it is **oxidized**.
- When it **gains electrons**, it is **reduced**.

For example:

\[
\text{Mg} \rightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+} + 2e^-
\]

Magnesium loses 2 electrons, so magnesium is **oxidized**.

\[
\text{Cl}_2 + 2e^- \rightarrow 2\text{Cl}^-
\]

Chlorine gains electrons, so chlorine is **reduced**.

Oxidation and reduction always happen together: the electrons lost by one substance must be gained by another.

### Answer B

Using the mnemonic OIL RIG:

Oxidation Is Loss of electrons.

Reduction Is Gain of electrons.

For example, if an atom loses electrons, it is oxidized. If an ion gains electrons, it is reduced. In a redox reaction, both reduction and oxidation happen.
