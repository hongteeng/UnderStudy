# Hong Ting 4's teaching rules

## Student-request types and recognition cues

- When a question asks about a material’s **physical properties**, first identify whether it concerns: (1) boiling or melting point, (2) electrical conductivity, or, less commonly, (3) hardness or strength. The first two are the main properties in this topic. (New_Recording_32.md)
- When a question asks for an explanation of a physical property, recognise that it requires a link from the material’s **structure**, to its **bonding**, and then to the stated physical property. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For an electrical-conductivity question about an ionic compound, identify the compound’s state. The answer depends on whether it is solid, molten, or aqueous. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For a covalent substance, first determine whether it has a **simple molecular structure** or a **giant covalent structure**, because the relevant bonding explanation differs. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Treat questions about graphite as special cases within giant covalent structures: they may test high boiling/melting point, softness, or electrical conductivity, each of which requires a different structural detail. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For questions comparing a pure metal with an alloy, focus on the arrangement and sizes of the constituent atoms and whether layers can slide past one another easily. (New_Recording_32.md)

- Recognise a question such as “compare the physical properties of carbon dioxide and diamond” as a structure-and-bonding question, even where the question does not explicitly use the words “structure and bonding.” (New_Recording_33.md)
- When a question uses the phrase “physical properties,” first consider melting/boiling point, then electrical conductivity; select a property that differs between the substances. (New_Recording_33.md)
- For a comparison question, identify the structures of both substances before explaining the bonding and its consequence for the physical property. (New_Recording_33.md)

- For a question asking for a similarity between silicon dioxide and diamond, begin by identifying the shared structure: both are “giant covalent structures.” (New_Recording_31.md)
- For electrical-conductivity questions about giant covalent structures, recognise that the explanation should focus on whether there are mobile ions or mobile electrons. (New_Recording_31.md)
- For a comparison involving graphite, distinguish it from diamond and silicon dioxide by considering its layers and mobile electrons. (New_Recording_31.md)

- Recognise questions asking students to use oxidation states to explain why a reaction is a redox reaction. (redox.md)
- Recognise questions asking students to explain why a compound is an oxidizing agent. (redox.md)
- Recognise questions requiring identification of redox through oxidation states. (redox.md)
- Recognise questions about electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer in redox. (redox.md)

## Tutor response approaches

- Use the recurring three-step flow: **first structure, secondly bonding, number three physical property**. Do not turn this into a compulsory checklist when the question asks for only one part. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Begin with the large organising idea that different materials have different physical properties, and that the material’s structure and bonding explain those properties. (New_Recording_32.md)
- After naming a bond, spell out the relevant attraction rather than relying only on the bond label. For ionic bonding, state the electrostatic attraction between the relevant cations and anions; for metallic bonding, state the attraction between metal cations and a sea of delocalised electrons. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For boiling- or melting-point explanations, state the relevant force or bond, then explain how much energy is required to overcome it, and finally state whether the boiling/melting point is high or low. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For conductivity explanations, identify the mobile charge carrier and whether it is free to move: ions in molten or aqueous ionic compounds, and delocalised electrons in metals and graphite. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Reuse concise template explanations for recurring property questions, but adapt the named particles to the substance in the question. For example, in NaCl, specify attraction between `Na⁺` and `Cl⁻` rather than only saying “cations and anions.” (New_Recording_32.md)
- Contrast closely related structures explicitly: simple molecular substances involve weak intermolecular forces between molecules, whereas giant covalent structures require strong covalent bonds between atoms to be overcome. (New_Recording_32.md)
- When teaching alloys, start with the problem of layers in a pure metal sliding past one another, then show how different-sized atoms disrupt the orderly arrangement and make sliding more difficult. (New_Recording_32.md)

- Use the order: “number one structure, number two bonding and then number three link the bonding to your physical property.” (New_Recording_33.md)
- For a two-mark comparison of carbon dioxide and diamond, give the structure comparison first, then compare bonding and link this to melting/boiling point. (New_Recording_33.md)
- When comparing physical properties, do not discuss a property if there is “no much difference there”; for diamond and carbon dioxide, both cannot conduct, so electrical conductivity is not used in this comparison. (New_Recording_33.md)
- Explain physical properties through structure and bonding even when the question does not explicitly ask for structure and bonding. (New_Recording_33.md)

- Start a similarity question by recalling the structural classification before giving properties: “First of all, we need to remember that they are both giant covalent structures.” (New_Recording_31.md)
- When explaining electrical conductivity, check both possible charge carriers: “mobile ions and mobile electrons.” (New_Recording_31.md)
- For diamond and silicon dioxide, conclude that they do not conduct electricity because they have “neither free moving ions nor free moving electrons.” (New_Recording_31.md)
- Keep the response focused on the substances named in the question; when the question is about SiO2 and diamond, do not go into graphite in unnecessary detail. (New_Recording_31.md)
- When answering a multiple-choice question, connect the completed reasoning to the option selected. (New_Recording_31.md)

- For oxidation-state explanations, use a standard answer structure that can be reused: identify the element, state that it is oxidized, and explain that its oxidation state increased between the named compounds. (redox.md)
- State the oxidation-state change numerically, including both the starting and ending oxidation states. (redox.md)
- Name the specific compound in which the element has each oxidation state. (redox.md)
- Teach oxidation-state identification alongside electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer. (redox.md)

## Required and preferred vocabulary

- Use **“giant ionic lattice structure”** for ionic compounds. The tutor treats this as a standard, reusable four-word phrase. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Describe ionic bonding as a **“strong electrostatic force of attraction between the cations and the anions.”** Where possible, replace the generic ions with the ions in the question. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Use **“simple molecular structure”** for discrete covalent molecules such as carbon dioxide, water, and nitrogen. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Use **“giant covalent structure”** for diamond, graphite, and silicon dioxide. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For simple molecular substances, use **“weak intermolecular forces of attraction.”** (New_Recording_32.md)
- For giant covalent substances, refer to **“strong covalent bonds between atoms,”** not bonds between molecules. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Use **“giant metallic lattice structure”** for metals. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Describe metallic bonding as **“strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the metal cations and a sea of delocalised electrons.”** Specify **metal cations**, not merely cations. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Prefer **“sea of delocalised electrons”** when describing metals; do not reduce this to the unqualified word “electrons.” (New_Recording_32.md)
- In conductivity explanations, identify **“free-moving”** or **“mobile”** charge carriers. Ionic compounds conduct through free-moving ions, while metals conduct through delocalised electrons. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For graphite’s softness, reuse the phrase **“layers of carbon atoms can slide past each other.”** (New_Recording_32.md)
- Define alloys as **mixtures**, not compounds: they are mixtures of metals and other elements. (New_Recording_32.md)

- Use “giant covalent structure” for diamond and “simple molecular structure” for carbon dioxide. (New_Recording_33.md)
- Use “a lot of energy is required to overcome the strong covalent bonds between carbon atoms in diamond.” (New_Recording_33.md)
- Use “very little energy is needed to overcome the weak intermolecular forces of attraction in carbon dioxide.” (New_Recording_33.md)
- State the property contrast as “very high boiling melting point” for diamond and “very low boiling melting point” for carbon dioxide. (New_Recording_33.md)

- Use “giant covalent structures” for diamond and silicon dioxide. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Contrast “giant covalent” with “simple molecular structure” when classifying covalent substances. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Use “high BPMP” or “high boiling melting point” for the shared boiling- and melting-point property of diamond and silicon dioxide. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Use “mobile ions” and “mobile electrons” when explaining electrical conduction. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Use “free moving ions” and “free moving electrons” in the final explanation for why diamond and silicon dioxide do not conduct. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Describe diamond as having a “tetrahedral structure.” (New_Recording_31.md)

- Use the terms “oxidation state,” “oxidized,” “redox reaction,” and “oxidizing agent” when addressing the corresponding question types. (redox.md)
- Use the reusable wording: “element X is oxidized because its oxidation state increased from plus 1 in … XCl to plus 2 in XO.” Adapt the element, oxidation states, and compounds to the question. (redox.md)
- Refer to the mnemonic “oil rig” when discussing electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer. (redox.md)

## Wording to avoid or qualify

- Do not write only **“ionic bonds”** when a full bonding explanation is required. Spell this out as the strong electrostatic force of attraction between the relevant cations and anions. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Do not call a giant covalent structure a **“giant molecular structure.”** Giant covalent substances are not made from discrete molecules; use **“giant covalent structure.”** (New_Recording_32.md)
- Do not say the strong covalent bonds in a giant covalent structure are **between molecules**. State that they are between atoms. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Do not explain the low boiling/melting point of a simple molecular substance by saying that covalent bonds are weak. The relevant attractions being overcome are the weak intermolecular forces between molecules. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Do not give an unconditional yes-or-no answer about ionic conductivity without stating the physical state. Ionic compounds conduct only when molten or aqueous, not when solid. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Do not treat ions and electrons as interchangeable charge carriers: ionic compounds conduct through mobile ions, whereas metals conduct through delocalised electrons. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Do not describe an alloy as a compound. Qualify it as a mixture of metals and other elements. (New_Recording_32.md)

- Do not treat a physical-properties question as separate from structure and bonding merely because the question does not use the words “structure and bonding.” (New_Recording_33.md)
- Do not include electrical conductivity in the diamond–carbon dioxide comparison, because “they both cannot conduct” and it does not provide a difference to compare. (New_Recording_33.md)

- Do not explain conductivity merely by saying that a substance is covalent; check explicitly for both mobile ions and mobile electrons. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Do not attribute mobile electrons to diamond or silicon dioxide; in the giant covalent structures discussed, graphite is the exception because of its layers. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Qualify the classification shortcut for unfamiliar covalent formulae as “most likely” simple molecular rather than an absolute rule. (New_Recording_31.md)

- Avoid stating only that an element was oxidized without giving the numerical oxidation-state increase. (redox.md)
- Avoid giving oxidation states without identifying the specific compounds in which the element has those oxidation states. (redox.md)

## Topic map and reusable explanations

- Organise the topic into three material types and four structures: ionic compounds have giant ionic lattice structures; covalent substances may have simple molecular or giant covalent structures; metals have giant metallic lattice structures. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For ionic compounds’ high boiling and melting points: a lot of energy is required to overcome the strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the cations and anions; therefore, ionic compounds have high boiling and melting points. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For ionic conductivity: in molten and aqueous states, ions are free to move and act as charge carriers, so the substance conducts electricity. In the solid state, ions vibrate about fixed positions and are not free to move, so it does not conduct. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For simple molecular substances’ low boiling and melting points: very little energy is required to overcome the weak intermolecular forces of attraction. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For simple molecular substances’ lack of conductivity: they have no free-moving ions or free-moving electrons. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For giant covalent substances’ high boiling and melting points: a lot of energy is required to overcome the strong covalent bonds between atoms. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Explain diamond’s hardness through strong covalent bonds throughout its structure, which require a lot of energy to overcome. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Explain graphite’s high boiling and melting points in the same way as diamond: strong covalent bonds must be overcome. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Explain graphite’s softness by its layered structure and weak forces between the layers: very little energy is required to overcome these weak forces, so layers of carbon atoms can slide past each other. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Explain graphite’s electrical conductivity by stating that each carbon atom is bonded to three other carbon atoms, leaving one delocalised electron per carbon atom free to conduct electricity. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Explain why diamond does not conduct electricity by stating that it has no free-moving electrons or ions. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For metals’ high boiling and melting points: a lot of energy is required to overcome the strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the metal cations and the sea of delocalised electrons. (New_Recording_32.md)
- For metals’ electrical conductivity: metals have a sea of delocalised electrons that can move and conduct electricity. (New_Recording_32.md)

- Do not generalise that all metals are soft; when explaining the malleability of a pure metal, state that layers of metal ions can slide past one another while metallic bonding is maintained. (New_Recording_32.md, tutor-edited)
- Explain why alloys are harder than the corresponding pure metals by stating that atoms of different sizes disrupt the orderly arrangement of metal atoms, making it more difficult for layers to slide past one another. (New_Recording_32.md)

- Physical-properties comparisons are approached through structure and bonding: different structures indicate that structure and bonding should be used to explain the comparison. (New_Recording_33.md)
- Diamond has a giant covalent structure, whereas carbon dioxide has a simple molecular structure. (New_Recording_33.md)
- In diamond, strong covalent bonds between carbon atoms require a lot of energy to overcome, linking to its high boiling/melting point. (New_Recording_33.md)
- In carbon dioxide, weak intermolecular forces of attraction require very little energy to overcome, linking to its low boiling/melting point. (New_Recording_33.md)
- For the stated two-mark question, the first mark comes from giving the structure; the second comes from comparing the bonding difference and therefore the physical property. (New_Recording_33.md)

- **Choosing a method**
  - If the question specifies a method, use that method. (New_Recording_30.md)
  - If it does not specify a method, use the method that is easiest for the reaction. (New_Recording_30.md)
  - Choose the method most suited to the scenario given. (New_Recording_30.md)
- **Oxidation state**
  - Oxidation state is universal and can be used regardless of the kind of reaction. (New_Recording_30.md)
  - It is the most common method specified in Paper 2. (New_Recording_30.md)
- **Oxygen and hydrogen transfer**
  - Oxygen transfer depends on oxygen being present in the reaction as a reactant or product. (New_Recording_30.md)
  - The same condition applies to hydrogen transfer and hydrogen. (New_Recording_30.md)
- **Electron transfer**
  - Electron transfer is especially easy to use when ions are present. (New_Recording_30.md)
  - For `I−` becoming `I2`, electron transfer is easier to use than hydrogen or oxygen transfer because the reaction contains neither H nor O. (New_Recording_30.md)

- Covalent structures are taught in two types: simple molecular and giant covalent. (New_Recording_31.md)
- CO2 is used as an example of a molecular substance. (New_Recording_31.md)
- The giant covalent structures learned are silicon dioxide, diamond, and graphite. (New_Recording_31.md)
- For an unfamiliar molecular formula, the tutor’s shortcut is that it will “most likely” be a simple molecular structure, since the giant covalent structures learned are limited to silicon dioxide, diamond, and graphite. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Diamond and silicon dioxide share giant covalent structure, high boiling and melting points, and lack of electrical conductivity. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Explain the lack of conduction as a structure-to-property link: they are covalent compounds with no mobile ions, and they also have no mobile electrons. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Graphite differs in electrical conductivity because it has mobile electrons associated with its layers. (New_Recording_31.md)

- Redox is presented as a fundamental concept for O-Level chemistry. (redox.md)
- Prioritise learning the oxidation-state rules when revising redox. (redox.md)
- Link redox understanding to later, harder topics such as electrochemistry. (redox.md)
- Explain oxidation using an oxidation-state increase, supported by the numerical change and the compounds involved. (redox.md)
- Cover redox identification through oxidation states as well as electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer. (redox.md)

## Analogies and examples to reuse

- Use pencil lead/graphite, diamond, oxygen, metals, water, and carbon dioxide as familiar materials whose differing physical properties motivate the link between structure, bonding, and properties. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Use sodium chloride, sodium sulfate, magnesium chloride, and magnesium nitrate as examples of ionic compounds with giant ionic lattice structures. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Use carbon dioxide, water, and nitrogen gas as examples of simple molecular substances. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Use diamond, graphite, and silicon dioxide as the three recurring examples of giant covalent structures. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Compare diamond and graphite to show that substances made only from carbon can have different hardness and conductivity because of their different structures. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Reuse the image of graphite as layers and layers of carbon atoms, with the layers able to slide past one another, when explaining its softness. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Compare graphite and metals through the shared layer-sliding idea, while keeping their bonding explanations distinct. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Use steel as an alloy example: iron and carbon. Use stainless steel as an example containing iron, nickel, and chromium. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Use carbon atoms in iron as the example of differently sized atoms disrupting an orderly metal arrangement and making layer-sliding more difficult. (New_Recording_32.md)
- The spoon example may be used to motivate why practical materials should resist bending or breaking and why alloys are used instead of some pure metals. (New_Recording_32.md)

- Use carbon dioxide and diamond as contrasting examples of a simple molecular structure and a giant covalent structure when teaching how structure and bonding explain melting/boiling point. (New_Recording_33.md)

- Use the informal paired-participant analogy: “you cannot have one guy being reduced and then don’t want to be oxidised. It doesn’t work like that.” (New_Recording_29.md)
- Use the three-reactant example to show that not every reactant necessarily changes: “one guy reduced, one guy oxidised and the other guy nothing happens.” (New_Recording_29.md)

- Use `I− becomes I2` as an example of an ionic reaction where electron transfer is a lot easier to use. (New_Recording_30.md)
- Contrast this example with hydrogen and oxygen transfer: the equation does not contain H or O, so those methods cannot be used for that specific equation. (New_Recording_30.md)

- Use CO2 as the example when introducing a simple molecular structure. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Use graphite as the contrast case when explaining that a giant covalent structure can conduct only when mobile electrons are present. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Use diamond’s tetrahedral structure as the structural example previously learned for diamond. (New_Recording_31.md)

- Use the illustrative oxidation-state explanation: “element X is oxidized because its oxidation state increased from plus 1 in for example XCl to plus 2 in XO.” (redox.md)
- Use “oil rig” as the mnemonic named for electron, hydrogen, and oxygen transfer in redox. (redox.md)

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- Pre-empt the idea that naming a bond alone explains a property. Students must connect the structure and fully described attraction to the energy required or to the availability of mobile charge carriers. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Pre-empt the idea that all covalent substances have the same structure. Covalent substances may be simple molecular or giant covalent. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Pre-empt the term **“giant molecular structure.”** Diamond, graphite, and silicon dioxide are giant covalent structures rather than collections of discrete molecules. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Pre-empt confusion between intermolecular forces and covalent bonds: weak intermolecular forces are used for simple molecular boiling/melting explanations, while strong covalent bonds between atoms are used for giant covalent structures. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Pre-empt the claim that solid ionic compounds conduct electricity. Their ions are in fixed positions and cannot act as mobile charge carriers. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Pre-empt the claim that ionic compounds never conduct. They conduct in molten and aqueous states because the ions are mobile. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Pre-empt confusion about graphite: it remains a giant covalent structure and has strong covalent bonds within its layers, even though weak forces occur between the layers. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Pre-empt the assumption that all giant covalent structures are electrical insulators. Graphite is the special conducting example because it has delocalised electrons. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Pre-empt confusion between ionic and metallic conduction: ionic compounds conduct through mobile ions, while metals conduct through mobile delocalised electrons. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Pre-empt the idea that alloys are compounds. Alloys are mixtures, and their different-sized atoms disrupt the regular arrangement found in a pure metal. (New_Recording_32.md)

- Pre-empt the idea that a question must explicitly say “structure and bonding” before structure and bonding should be used: physical-properties questions should still be explained using structure and bonding. (New_Recording_33.md)
- Pre-empt choosing electrical conductivity automatically for every physical-properties comparison: first check whether it differs between the two substances. (New_Recording_33.md)

- Pre-empt the idea that all covalent substances have the same structure by distinguishing simple molecular substances from giant covalent structures. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Pre-empt the idea that electrical conduction has only one route: the tutor identifies two routes, mobile ions and mobile electrons. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Pre-empt the idea that all giant covalent structures conduct: diamond and silicon dioxide do not conduct, whereas graphite has mobile electrons. (New_Recording_31.md)

- Pre-empt incomplete oxidation-state explanations: students need to state both the numerical increase and the specific compounds involved, not merely say that oxidation occurred. (redox.md)

## Syllabus boundaries

- Pitch the material at O-level chemistry and present bonding and structure as a fundamental topic supporting later chemistry topics. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Keep the main scope to three material types—ionic compounds, covalent substances, and metals—and four structures because covalent substances have two structural types. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Prioritise boiling/melting point and electrical conductivity as the two main physical properties. Treat hardness or softness as an occasional extension, especially for graphite, metals, and alloys. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Use kinetic particle theory only to support the movement and arrangement of ions in different physical states when explaining ionic conductivity. (New_Recording_32.md)
- Limit the named giant covalent examples in this lesson to diamond, graphite, and silicon dioxide. (New_Recording_32.md)

- This source addresses comparing the physical properties of carbon dioxide and diamond, specifically structure, bonding, melting/boiling point, and electrical conductivity. (New_Recording_33.md)

- For the silicon dioxide-and-diamond comparison, answer through their shared giant covalent classification and resulting properties rather than relying on an unspecified detailed structure for silicon dioxide. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Keep graphite detail limited when graphite is not the substance being assessed; use it only as a brief conductivity contrast where helpful. (New_Recording_31.md)

- The stated level is Chemistry O-Level. (redox.md)
- Electrochemistry is identified as a harder topic for which redox is foundational, rather than being taught in this redox segment. (redox.md)
