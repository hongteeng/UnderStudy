**Question:** Explain why metals conduct electricity and why they are malleable.

### UnderStudy answer

Metals have a giant metallic lattice structure.

For electrical conductivity, there is a sea of delocalised electrons. These delocalised electrons are free-moving, so they can move through the metal and conduct electricity.

### Generic baseline answer

Metals have a special type of bonding called **metallic bonding**.

### Why metals conduct electricity
In a metal, the outer electrons are not held tightly by individual atoms. Instead, they form a “sea” of **delocalised electrons** that can move freely through the metal.

When a voltage is applied, these mobile electrons flow through the metal and carry electric charge. That is why metals such as copper and aluminium are good electrical conductors.

Metal atoms are arranged in layers, held together by attraction between the positive metal ions and the sea of delocalised electrons.

When a force is applied, the layers of atoms can **slide past each other** without the metal breaking. The mobile electrons still hold the ions together even after the layers move.

This means metals can be hammered into sheets or shaped into different forms. This property is called **malleability**.

## Question 13: q13_transition_metal_reactivity_trends

**Question:** What are the trends in reactivity across the transition metals?

### UnderStudy answer

This goes beyond the reviewed material, so do double-check it against your syllabus.

Across a transition-metal series, reactivity generally decreases from left to right, although the trend is not perfectly regular.

- Metals on the left, such as scandium and titanium, are generally more reactive.
- Metals towards the right, such as copper, are much less reactive.
- This is because the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons generally becomes stronger across the period, so electrons are harder to lose.
- The trend is irregular because transition metals can form different ions with different oxidation states.

Overall, transition metals are usually less reactive than Group 1 and Group 2 metals.

### Generic baseline answer

Transition metals are generally **less reactive than Group 1 and Group 2 metals**, but their reactivity varies a lot because of their electron structures and oxide coatings.

### Across a transition series (left to right)
There is a **general decrease in reactivity** from left to right, although it is not perfectly smooth.

- Metals on the **left** (such as scandium, titanium, and vanadium) tend to lose electrons more easily, so they are usually more reactive.
- Metals toward the **right** (such as copper, silver, platinum, and gold) are less likely to lose electrons and are much less reactive.

For example, in the first transition series:

**Ti / V / Cr / Mn → Fe → Co → Ni → Cu**

reactivity generally decreases overall, with some irregularities.

### Why the trend is irregular
Unlike the main-group elements, transition metals have electrons in both the outer shell and a nearby \(d\) subshell. This means:

- several oxidation states are possible,
- the energy needed to remove electrons changes unevenly,
- metal–metal bonding strength also changes across the series.

So there is no perfectly simple pattern.

### Reactions with water and acids
- Most transition metals **do not react rapidly with cold water**.
- More reactive ones may react with steam or acids, releasing hydrogen gas.
- Metals such as **iron, nickel, and cobalt** react slowly with dilute acids.
- **Copper, silver, gold, and platinum** do not react with dilute non-oxidising acids.

### Oxide layers and passivation
Some metals seem less reactive than they really are because they form a protective oxide layer.

- **Aluminium** and **titanium** form tough oxide coatings.
- **Chromium** also forms a protective oxide layer, which is why it is used in stainless steel.
- This protection is called **passivation**.

### Down a transition-metal group
There is not one simple rule down every group. However, heavier transition metals are often **less easily oxidised** and can be more “noble.” For example, in Group 11:

\[
\text{Cu} > \text{Ag} > \text{Au}
\]

in reactivity, so gold is extremely unreactive.

### Overall summary
- Transition metals are moderately reactive compared with the very reactive Group 1 and 2 metals.
- Reactivity generally **decreases from left to right** across a transition series.
- The trend is **irregular** because \(d\)-electrons affect bonding and electron loss.
- Metals such as copper, silver, gold, and platinum are among the least reactive.
- Oxide coatings can make metals like titanium and chromium appear less reactive.

Oxidation and reduction always happen together: the electrons lost by one substance must be gained by another.
