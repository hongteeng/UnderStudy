# Draft tutor rules: Giant covalent structures — diamond and silicon dioxide

## Student-request types and recognition cues

- For a question asking for a similarity between silicon dioxide and diamond, begin by identifying the shared structure: both are “giant covalent structures.” (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- For electrical-conductivity questions about giant covalent structures, recognise that the explanation should focus on whether there are mobile ions or mobile electrons. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- For a comparison involving graphite, distinguish it from diamond and silicon dioxide by considering its layers and mobile electrons. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)

## Tutor response approaches

- Start a similarity question by recalling the structural classification before giving properties: “First of all, we need to remember that they are both giant covalent structures.” (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- When explaining electrical conductivity, check both possible charge carriers: “mobile ions and mobile electrons.” (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- For diamond and silicon dioxide, conclude that they do not conduct electricity because they have “neither free moving ions nor free moving electrons.” (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Keep the response focused on the substances named in the question; when the question is about SiO2 and diamond, do not go into graphite in unnecessary detail. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- When answering a multiple-choice question, connect the completed reasoning to the option selected. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)

## Required and preferred vocabulary

- Use “giant covalent structures” for diamond and silicon dioxide. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Contrast “giant covalent” with “simple molecular structure” when classifying covalent substances. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Use “high BPMP” or “high boiling melting point” for the shared boiling- and melting-point property of diamond and silicon dioxide. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Use “mobile ions” and “mobile electrons” when explaining electrical conduction. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Use “free moving ions” and “free moving electrons” in the final explanation for why diamond and silicon dioxide do not conduct. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Describe diamond as having a “tetrahedral structure.” (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)

## Wording to avoid or qualify

- Do not explain conductivity merely by saying that a substance is covalent; check explicitly for both mobile ions and mobile electrons. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Do not attribute mobile electrons to diamond or silicon dioxide; in the giant covalent structures discussed, graphite is the exception because of its layers. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Qualify the classification shortcut for unfamiliar covalent formulae as “most likely” simple molecular rather than an absolute rule. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)

## Topic map and reusable explanations

- Covalent structures are taught in two types: simple molecular and giant covalent. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- CO2 is used as an example of a molecular substance. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- The giant covalent structures learned are silicon dioxide, diamond, and graphite. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- For an unfamiliar molecular formula, the tutor’s shortcut is that it will “most likely” be a simple molecular structure, since the giant covalent structures learned are limited to silicon dioxide, diamond, and graphite. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Diamond and silicon dioxide share giant covalent structure, high boiling and melting points, and lack of electrical conductivity. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Explain the lack of conduction as a structure-to-property link: they are covalent compounds with no mobile ions, and they also have no mobile electrons. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Graphite differs in electrical conductivity because it has mobile electrons associated with its layers. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)

## Analogies and examples to reuse

- Use CO2 as the example when introducing a simple molecular structure. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Use graphite as the contrast case when explaining that a giant covalent structure can conduct only when mobile electrons are present. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Use diamond’s tetrahedral structure as the structural example previously learned for diamond. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- Pre-empt the idea that all covalent substances have the same structure by distinguishing simple molecular substances from giant covalent structures. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Pre-empt the idea that electrical conduction has only one route: the tutor identifies two routes, mobile ions and mobile electrons. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Pre-empt the idea that all giant covalent structures conduct: diamond and silicon dioxide do not conduct, whereas graphite has mobile electrons. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)

## Syllabus boundaries

- For the silicon dioxide-and-diamond comparison, answer through their shared giant covalent classification and resulting properties rather than relying on an unspecified detailed structure for silicon dioxide. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
- Keep graphite detail limited when graphite is not the substance being assessed; use it only as a brief conductivity contrast where helpful. (sio2_diamond_similarity.md)
