# Draft tutor rules: Chemical bonding and structure

## Review status

Draft extracted from one O-level chemistry lesson. The tutor’s central explanatory sequence is: **structure → bonding → physical property**. Three drafted rules require review because their wording could overgeneralise or mislead a student.

## Student-request types and recognition cues

- When a student asks about a material’s **physical properties**, prioritise boiling/melting point and electrical conductivity; discuss hardness or strength only sometimes. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- When a student asks for an explanation of a property, organise the response in three steps: identify the **structure**, describe the **bonding**, then link this to the **physical property**. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- When a student asks whether an ionic compound conducts electricity, distinguish its state: it conducts only when aqueous or molten, not when solid. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- When a student asks about a covalent substance’s structure, first distinguish **simple molecular structure** from **giant covalent structure**. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- When a student asks about graphite, treat it as a special giant covalent structure and separately address its high boiling/melting point, softness, and electrical conductivity. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- When a student asks why a metal conducts electricity, explain conduction using its sea of delocalised electrons, in contrast with ionic compounds, which conduct through free-moving ions. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- When a student asks why an alloy is harder than a metal, link different-sized added elements to disruption of the orderly arrangement of metal atoms, making layers difficult to slide past each other. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)

## Tutor response approaches

- Use the recurring answer flow: **firstly structure, secondly bonding, number three the physical property**. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Begin property explanations by identifying the relevant particles or forces, then state the energy required to overcome them and the resulting boiling/melting point. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For electrical conductivity, identify whether there are mobile charge carriers, and specify whether these are ions or electrons. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For ionic compounds, name the structure as a **giant ionic lattice structure**, then describe bonding as a strong electrostatic force of attraction between the relevant cations and anions. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For simple molecular substances, distinguish weak intermolecular forces of attraction from covalent bonds within molecules. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For giant covalent substances, describe strong covalent bonds **between atoms**, rather than forces between molecules. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Use familiar examples to establish categories: sodium chloride, sodium sulphate, magnesium chloride, and magnesium nitrate for ionic compounds; carbon dioxide, water, and nitrogen gas for simple molecular substances; diamond, graphite, and silicon dioxide for giant covalent structures. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For metals, identify a **giant metallic lattice structure**, then explain the strong electrostatic forces of attraction between metal cations and a sea of delocalised electrons. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)

## Required and preferred vocabulary

- Use **giant ionic lattice structure** as the standard structure term for ionic compounds. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- In an exam explanation, do not merely write “ionic bonds”; spell this out as **strong electrostatic force(s) of attraction between the cations and anions**. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Adapt the ions to the question context; for sodium chloride, write “between Na⁺ and Cl⁻.” (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Use **simple molecular structure** for substances made up of discrete molecules. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Use **giant covalent structure**, not “giant molecular structure,” for diamond, graphite, and silicon dioxide. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For simple molecular substances, use **weak intermolecular forces of attraction** when explaining low boiling/melting point. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For giant covalent structures, use **strong covalent bonds** and specify that they are **between atoms**. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Use **giant metallic lattice structure** for metals. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For metallic bonding, write **strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the metal cations and a sea of delocalised electrons**. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Specify **metal cations**, not merely “cations,” and a **sea of delocalised electrons**, rather than merely “electrons.” (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For graphite’s conductivity, use: “One carbon atom is bonded to three carbon atoms. There is one free moving electron per carbon atom, free to conduct electricity.” (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For graphite’s softness, use: “Layers of carbon atoms can slide past each other, therefore graphite is soft.” (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- For metallic conductivity, use: “Metals have a sea of delocalised electrons, therefore metals can conduct electricity.” (bonding_structure_lesson.md)

## Wording to avoid or qualify

- Do not call a giant covalent structure a **giant molecular structure**: things with a giant covalent structure “are not molecules.” (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Do not explain a giant covalent substance’s high boiling/melting point using weak intermolecular forces; use strong covalent bonds between atoms. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Do not explain simple molecular substances’ low boiling/melting point by saying covalent bonds are overcome; use weak intermolecular forces of attraction. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Do not state that ionic compounds conduct electricity in every state; qualify that they conduct only in aqueous and molten states. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Do not confuse the charge carriers: ionic compounds conduct via free-moving ions, whereas metals conduct via free-moving electrons. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Do not describe graphite as behaving exactly like diamond in every property: their boiling/melting point explanation is the same, but their softness and conductivity differ. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)

## Topic map and reusable explanations

- The topic concerns materials, their structures and bonding, and how these explain physical properties. The most important physical properties are boiling/melting point and electrical conductivity; softness is discussed specifically for graphite and metals. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Ionic compounds have a giant ionic lattice structure. Their high boiling/melting point is explained by: “A lot of energy is required to overcome the strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the cations and anions, therefore ionic compounds have a high boiling melting point.” (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Ionic compounds conduct electricity in aqueous and molten states because they have free-moving/mobile ions, which are charge carriers. In the solid state, ions vibrate about fixed positions and are not free to move. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Simple molecular structures consist of many discrete molecules. They have low boiling/melting point because very little energy is required to overcome weak intermolecular forces of attraction. They cannot conduct electricity because they have no free-moving ions or free-moving electrons. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Giant covalent structures are one large arrangement of atoms connected to each other. Diamond and silicon dioxide have high boiling/melting point because much energy is needed to overcome strong covalent bonds. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Diamond cannot conduct electricity because it has no free-moving electrons or ions. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Graphite has a high boiling/melting point because strong covalent bonds must be overcome. It can conduct electricity because each carbon atom is bonded to three carbon atoms, leaving one free-moving electron per carbon atom. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Metals have a giant metallic lattice structure. Their electrical conductivity is due to the sea of delocalised electrons. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Alloys are mixtures, not compounds. They are mixtures of metals and other elements; for example, steel is iron plus carbon, while stainless steel contains iron, nickel, and chromium. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- In an alloy, atoms of different sizes disrupt the orderly arrangement of metal atoms, making it difficult for layers to slide past each other; therefore alloys are harder. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)

## Analogies and examples to reuse

- Use pencil lead/graphite, diamond, oxygen, and metals as initial examples of different materials with different physical properties. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Compare water’s boiling/melting point with diamond’s, or compare carbon dioxide’s electrical conductivity with graphite’s or sodium’s, to frame structure–property comparisons. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Use graphite’s visible “layers and layers of carbon atoms” as the structural image for explaining why its layers can slide past each other. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Use the spoon example to motivate why metals may need to be made harder: “we don’t want to hold a spoon and then suddenly like it bends.” (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Use carbon and iron as the example of different-sized atoms in steel: carbon has a different size from iron and disrupts the orderly arrangement. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- Students may give only the label “ionic bonds” in an exam answer; pre-empt this by requiring the full electrostatic-attraction description and the named ions in context. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Students may assume ionic compounds always conduct; pre-empt this by contrasting mobile ions in aqueous/molten states with fixed-position ions in the solid state. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Students may confuse simple molecular substances with giant covalent structures; distinguish many discrete molecules from one large structure of connected atoms. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Students may say “giant molecular structure”; correct this to **giant covalent structure**. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Students may use the same bonding explanation for simple molecular and giant covalent substances; distinguish weak intermolecular forces from strong covalent bonds. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Students may assume all giant covalent structures have the same conductivity; pre-empt this by contrasting diamond, which cannot conduct, with graphite, which can. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Students may confuse the charge carriers in ionic compounds and metals; contrast mobile ions with delocalised electrons. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- Students may call alloys compounds; correct this by stating that alloys are mixtures. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)

## Syllabus boundaries

- The lesson is presented as an **O-level** chemistry lesson on bonding and structure. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- The lesson groups substances into ionic compounds, covalent compounds, and metals; covalent compounds are further divided into simple molecular and giant covalent structures. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- The lesson focuses mainly on boiling/melting point and electrical conductivity. Softness is specified as relevant particularly to graphite and metals, rather than ionic compounds. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- The giant covalent examples covered are diamond, graphite, and silicon dioxide. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
- The alloy examples covered are steel/iron plus carbon and stainless steel/iron with nickel and chromium. (bonding_structure_lesson.md)
