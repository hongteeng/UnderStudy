# Draft tutor rules: Redox reactions—choosing a method

## Student-request types and recognition cues

- **Choosing between redox-identification methods:** Recognise this when a student asks when to use oxidation state versus electron transfer to decide whether a reaction is redox. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- **An unspecified method:** If the question does not specify a particular method, the student may use whichever method they want. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- **A question specifying a method:** In Paper 2, questions usually specify use of oxidation states; follow the method specified in the question. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- **Reactions involving ions:** Electron transfer is particularly easy to use when the reaction contains ions, for example, `I−` becoming `I2`. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)

## Tutor response approaches

- Start by checking whether the question specifies a particular method. If it does not, tell the student to choose the method that is easiest or most suited to the scenario. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Recall the four redox methods: oxidation state, electron transfer, hydrogen transfer, and oxygen transfer. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Present oxidation state as the universal method: it can be used regardless of the kind of reaction. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Link the choice of method to what appears in the equation: use oxygen transfer only where oxygen is present as a reactant or product; apply the same idea for hydrogen transfer. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- For ionic reactions, demonstrate why electron transfer may be the simplest method by identifying an ionic change, such as `I−` becoming `I2`. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Explain why unsuitable methods cannot be used for a particular equation—for example, do not use hydrogen or oxygen transfer where the reaction contains neither H nor O. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)

## Required and preferred vocabulary

- Use the terms **oxidation state**, **electron transfer**, **hydrogen transfer**, and **oxygen transfer** for the four methods of identifying redox. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Use **O.S.** as the abbreviated form for oxidation state where appropriate. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Refer to whether a reaction contains oxygen or hydrogen as a **reactant or product** when deciding whether oxygen or hydrogen transfer applies. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Use the ionic example notation `I− becomes I2` when illustrating electron transfer. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)

## Wording to avoid or qualify

- Do not present oxygen transfer as applicable to every reaction; it is applicable only if the reaction contains oxygen as a reactant or product. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Do not present hydrogen transfer as applicable to every reaction; apply the same condition for hydrogen. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Do not use hydrogen transfer or oxygen transfer for an equation that contains no H or O, respectively. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Where a question specifies a particular redox method, do not substitute a different method just because it is easier. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)

## Topic map and reusable explanations

- **Choosing a method**
  - If the question specifies a method, use that method. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
  - If it does not specify a method, use the method that is easiest for the reaction. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
  - Choose the method most suited to the scenario given. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- **Oxidation state**
  - Oxidation state is universal and can be used regardless of the kind of reaction. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
  - It is the most common method specified in Paper 2. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- **Oxygen and hydrogen transfer**
  - Oxygen transfer depends on oxygen being present in the reaction as a reactant or product. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
  - The same condition applies to hydrogen transfer and hydrogen. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- **Electron transfer**
  - Electron transfer is especially easy to use when ions are present. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
  - For `I−` becoming `I2`, electron transfer is easier to use than hydrogen or oxygen transfer because the reaction contains neither H nor O. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)

## Analogies and examples to reuse

- Use `I− becomes I2` as an example of an ionic reaction where electron transfer is a lot easier to use. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Contrast this example with hydrogen and oxygen transfer: the equation does not contain H or O, so those methods cannot be used for that specific equation. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- Students may think they must always use one particular redox method. Clarify that, unless the question specifies a method, they can choose the one that is easiest for them. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Students may try to use oxygen or hydrogen transfer even when the relevant element is absent. Explain that the reaction must contain oxygen or hydrogen, respectively. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- Students may overlook electron transfer as the simplest approach in an ionic equation. Prompt them to check whether ions are present. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)

## Syllabus boundaries

- The transcript refers specifically to Paper 2, where oxidation states are usually the most commonly specified method. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
- The lesson addresses selecting among oxidation state, electron transfer, hydrogen transfer, and oxygen transfer for identifying redox reactions. (choosing_oxidation_method.md)
