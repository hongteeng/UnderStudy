# Draft tutor rules: Choosing a method to identify redox reactions

## Student-request types and recognition cues

- **Choosing between redox methods:** Recognise questions such as “How do I know when to use oxidation state, or when to use electron transfer?” The student is asking how to select a method for determining whether a reaction is redox. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- **Method explicitly specified:** If the question instructs the student to use a particular method, follow that instruction. Paper 2 questions commonly specify oxidation states. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- **Method not specified:** If no method is specified, the student may use whichever valid method is easiest and most suited to the reaction given. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)

## Tutor response approaches

- Begin by checking whether the question specifies a method. If it does, use that method; if it does not, choose the easiest suitable method. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- Present the four available methods: oxidation state, electron transfer, hydrogen transfer, and oxygen transfer. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- Explain that oxidation state is the universal option and can be used regardless of the kind of reaction. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- Then test the other methods against the reaction: look for oxygen when considering oxygen transfer, hydrogen when considering hydrogen transfer, and ions when considering electron transfer. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- End with the general principle: “choose a method that is most suited to the scenario that you are given.” (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)

## Required and preferred vocabulary

- Use the four method names accurately: **oxidation state**, **electron transfer**, **hydrogen transfer**, and **oxygen transfer**. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- The tutor abbreviates oxidation state as **O.S.** and describes it as the most common method specified in Paper 2. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- When discussing method choice, use the wording **“most suited to the scenario”** or **“easiest for you”** when the question has not prescribed a method. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)

## Wording to avoid or qualify

- Do not imply that the student must always use oxidation states when the question does not specify a method; any valid method may be used if it suits the reaction. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- Do not suggest oxygen transfer for a reaction containing no oxygen, or hydrogen transfer for a reaction containing no hydrogen. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- Do not make the choice depend solely on whether the question is an MCQ; the tutor retracts that framing and instead bases the choice on the stated instruction and the reaction itself. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)

## Topic map and reusable explanations

- There are four methods for identifying redox: oxidation state, electron transfer, hydrogen transfer, and oxygen transfer. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- **Oxidation state:** This is universal and can always be used, regardless of the type of reaction. It is also the method most commonly specified in Paper 2. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- **Oxygen transfer:** Consider this when oxygen appears as a reactant or product. It cannot be used for a reaction containing no oxygen. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- **Hydrogen transfer:** Apply the corresponding suitability check for hydrogen. It cannot be used for a reaction containing no hydrogen. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- **Electron transfer:** This method is especially easy to use when the reaction contains ions. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- The decision sequence is: obey any specified method; otherwise inspect the equation and choose the easiest applicable method. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)

## Analogies and examples to reuse

- Use `I⁻ becomes I₂` as an example where electron transfer is easier to use because an ion is involved. The same equation cannot be analysed through hydrogen transfer or oxygen transfer because it contains neither H nor O. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- Pre-empt the belief that there is one compulsory method for every unspecified redox question: when no method is prescribed, the student can choose a valid method suited to the reaction. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- Pre-empt the belief that all four methods apply to every equation: oxygen and hydrogen transfer depend on the relevant element being present, whereas oxidation state is universal. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
- Pre-empt overlooking electron transfer in ionic reactions: if ions are present, electron transfer may be the easiest method. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)

## Syllabus boundaries

- This guidance concerns selecting a method to determine whether a reaction is redox or whether something is oxidising, with a specific reference to Paper 2 question wording. (data/teachers/hong-ting-4/transcripts/New_Recording_30.md)
