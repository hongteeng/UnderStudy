# Draft tutor rules: Comparing physical properties using structure and bonding

## Review status

Draft extracted from one short lesson transcript. One rule has been flagged for tutor review because its wording about “boiling/melting point” may be scientifically imprecise if applied literally outside the intended curriculum context.

## Student-request types and recognition cues

- Treat a question that asks students to “compare the physical properties” of substances as a structure-and-bonding question, even if the question does not explicitly use the words “structure and bonding.” (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- When comparing physical properties, first identify a property that differs between the two substances; the tutor’s stated priority is melting/boiling point, followed by electrical conductivity. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- Do not discuss a physical property if there is “no much difference there”; in the diamond/carbon dioxide comparison, both cannot conduct electricity, so electrical conductivity is not used. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)

## Tutor response approaches

- For structure-and-bonding comparisons, use this order: “number one structure, number two bonding and then number three link the bonding to your physical property.” (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- Begin by naming and contrasting the structures of both substances. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- Then compare the energy needed to overcome the relevant forces or bonds, and link this difference to the physical property. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- For a two-mark comparison question, the tutor identifies the first mark as the structure and the second mark as comparing the difference in bonding “and therefore their physical property.” (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- In the diamond/carbon dioxide comparison, use the contrast between strong covalent bonds in diamond and weak intermolecular forces of attraction in carbon dioxide. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)

## Required and preferred vocabulary

- Use the structural terms “giant covalent structure” for diamond and “simple molecular structure” for carbon dioxide. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- Use “strong covalent bonds between carbon atoms in diamond.” (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- Use “weak intermolecular forces of attraction in carbon dioxide.” (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- Use energy language: “a lot of energy is required to overcome” the strong covalent bonds in diamond, whereas “very little energy is needed to overcome” the weak intermolecular forces in carbon dioxide. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- Use “physical properties,” “structure and bonding,” and explicitly link bonding to the physical property being compared. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)

## Wording to avoid or qualify

> ⚠ **REVIEW REQUIRED** — Source: `co2_diamond_comparison.md`
> **RULE AS DRAFTED:** - For the diamond/carbon dioxide comparison, state that “diamond has a very high boiling melting point and carbon dioxide has a very low boiling melting point.” (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
> **ISSUE WITH THIS RULE:** This wording combines boiling point and melting point as though they are a single property and may be scientifically misleading if interpreted literally, particularly for carbon dioxide under normal atmospheric pressure. It could lead students to state an imprecise property rather than explaining the energy required for a change of state.
> **SUGGESTED REVISION:** - For the diamond/carbon dioxide comparison, compare the much greater energy required to change the state of diamond with the much smaller energy required to change the state of carbon dioxide, linking this to strong covalent bonds versus weak intermolecular forces. (co2_diamond_comparison.md, tutor-edited)

## Topic map and reusable explanations

- **Question trigger: physical properties** → think about structure and bonding. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- **First comparison step: structure** → diamond has a giant covalent structure; carbon dioxide has a simple molecular structure. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- **Second comparison step: bonding/forces** → diamond has strong covalent bonds between carbon atoms; carbon dioxide has weak intermolecular forces of attraction. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- **Third comparison step: consequence** → more energy is required to overcome diamond’s covalent bonds, while little energy is needed to overcome carbon dioxide’s intermolecular forces. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- When considering which physical property to compare, think of melting/boiling point first, then electrical conductivity, and select one that is different for the substances given. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)

## Analogies and examples to reuse

- Use diamond and carbon dioxide as the contrasting example of a giant covalent structure versus a simple molecular structure. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- Use the energy comparison as the explanatory contrast: “a lot of energy” for strong covalent bonds in diamond versus “very little energy” for weak intermolecular forces in carbon dioxide. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- Pre-empt the idea that a question must explicitly say “structure and bonding” before students should use structure and bonding in their explanation. The tutor stresses that physical-properties questions should prompt this thinking regardless of the wording. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- Pre-empt discussing every possible physical property: first check whether the property is actually different. For diamond and carbon dioxide, electrical conductivity is not a useful comparison because both cannot conduct. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
- Pre-empt giving a property statement without a bonding explanation: link the bonds or forces that must be overcome to the resulting physical property. (co2_diamond_comparison.md)
