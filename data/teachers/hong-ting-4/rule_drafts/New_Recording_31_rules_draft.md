# Draft tutor rules: Similarities between silicon dioxide and diamond

## Student-request types and recognition cues

- Recognise a comparison request when the student asks for “a similarity between silicon dioxide and diamond.” Begin by identifying the shared structure type before comparing properties. (New_Recording_31.md)
- For a multiple-choice similarity question, test the possible shared properties and then identify the matching answer option. (New_Recording_31.md)

## Tutor response approaches

- Start with the classification: silicon dioxide and diamond are both giant covalent structures. The tutor says this “should be the first thing that comes to mind.” (New_Recording_31.md)
- After classifying the substances, compare relevant properties such as melting/boiling point and electrical conductivity. (New_Recording_31.md)
- For electrical conductivity, check both possible charge carriers: mobile ions and mobile electrons. Conclude that diamond and silicon dioxide conduct by neither route. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Keep the explanation focused on the substances named in the question. Mention graphite only to clarify the exception, without expanding into an unrelated discussion of graphite. (New_Recording_31.md)
- When answering a similarity question about silicon dioxide and diamond, suitable shared properties include high melting and boiling points and inability to conduct electricity. (New_Recording_31.md)

## Required and preferred vocabulary

- Use the classification “giant covalent structures” for both silicon dioxide and diamond. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Use “simple molecular” and “giant covalent” as the two structure categories discussed for covalently bonded substances. (New_Recording_31.md)
- State that diamond and silicon dioxide have “high boiling [and] melting point,” or high boiling and melting points. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Explain the lack of electrical conductivity using “mobile ions” and “mobile electrons,” or the tutor’s final phrasing: “neither free moving ions nor free moving electrons.” (New_Recording_31.md)
- Use “tetrahedral structure” when referring to the structure of diamond. (New_Recording_31.md)

## Wording to avoid or qualify

- Do not say only that diamond and silicon dioxide “cannot conduct.” Add the reason that they have neither free-moving ions nor free-moving electrons. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Do not apply the non-conducting statement to every giant covalent structure without qualification: graphite is the stated exception because it has mobile electrons. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Keep the heuristic that an unfamiliar covalent substance is likely simple molecular explicitly within the taught syllabus context; the lesson bases it on silicon dioxide, diamond, and graphite being the giant covalent structures the students have learned. (New_Recording_31.md)

## Topic map and reusable explanations

- Covalent substances in this lesson are organised into two structure categories: simple molecular and giant covalent. Carbon dioxide is used as an example of a simple molecular substance; silicon dioxide, diamond, and graphite are the taught giant covalent examples. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Silicon dioxide and diamond share a giant covalent structure classification. This provides the starting point for predicting their shared properties. (New_Recording_31.md)
- The taught giant covalent examples have high melting and boiling points. Therefore, silicon dioxide and diamond both have high melting and boiling points. (New_Recording_31.md)
- To decide whether a substance conducts electricity, check for either mobile ions or mobile electrons. Silicon dioxide and diamond have neither, so they cannot conduct electricity. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Graphite is the exception among the taught giant covalent structures because it has mobile electrons associated with its layered structure. (New_Recording_31.md)

## Analogies and examples to reuse

- Use molecular carbon dioxide as the example of a simple molecular structure. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Use silicon dioxide, diamond, and graphite as the three giant covalent structures taught in this lesson context. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Use graphite as a contrast when explaining why “giant covalent” does not automatically mean “cannot conduct electricity.” (New_Recording_31.md)

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- Do not assume that every giant covalent structure is electrically non-conducting. Graphite has mobile electrons and is the stated exception. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Do not look only for mobile electrons when explaining conductivity. The tutor checks both possible routes: mobile ions and mobile electrons. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Do not treat silicon dioxide and diamond as simple molecular substances; both are giant covalent structures. (New_Recording_31.md)

## Syllabus boundaries

- The giant covalent structures identified as learned are silicon dioxide, diamond, and graphite. Keep classification heuristics tied to this taught set. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Do not expand into detailed graphite teaching when the question only asks about silicon dioxide and diamond; use graphite only as the relevant conductivity exception. (New_Recording_31.md)
- Diamond’s tetrahedral structure is treated as previously learned rather than re-taught in detail here. (New_Recording_31.md)
